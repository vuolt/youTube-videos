## Cold open
1. Imagine your phone buzzes with a text from yourself.
2. It says: "Don't send this message tomorrow."
3. You check the timestamp. It was sent tomorrow.
4. So you do what it says. Tomorrow comes, and you don't send it.
5. But then, who sent the message you got today?
6. If it arrived, it was never sent. And if it was never sent, it could never have arrived. That's a paradox.
7. But if faster-than-light travel were possible, these kinds of paradoxes would become reality.
8. Luckily, physics has a rule against it: nothing can travel faster than light.

## What everyone believes
9. You probably knew that already, and so does every sci-fi writer. So their shortcuts are built never to really go faster than light.
10. Star Trek's warp drive bends space around the ship.
11. In Star Wars and Halo, ships slip into another dimension, where the trip is shorter.
12. Stargate and Interstellar use wormholes: tunnels through space.
13. So the usual verdict is: clever, but it doesn't break physics.
14. And it's true. They don't break the speed limit.
15. But every one of them breaks something bigger: cause and effect. Every one of them is secretly a time machine.

## The race
16. To see why, start with the problem they're all solving: space is enormous.
17. Even at light speed, the nearest star is more than four years away. Crossing our galaxy takes a hundred thousand years.
18. And all these shortcuts come down to two tricks.
19. Trick one: move space, not the ship. Squeeze space together in front of the ship, and stretch it out behind.
20. The ship sits still in a calm bubble, carried along like someone standing on a moving walkway.
21. This isn't just fiction. In 1994, the physicist Miguel Alcubierre showed that a warp drive like this works on paper, inside Einstein's own equations.
22. Trick two: take a shorter road. A wormhole is a tunnel linking two distant places. You don't go faster. The trip is just shorter.
23. Both need something called "negative energy", and nobody knows if enough of it can exist. But let's say it can.
24. Picture a race. You fire a beam of light at a distant star, and at the same moment, you set off by warp drive, or through a wormhole.
25. You win. You reach the star before the light does.
26. That's the whole point of a shortcut. And that's exactly where cause and effect breaks.
27. Because of a strange fact about our universe: there is no single "now".
28. What's happening "right now" on a distant star depends on how you're moving.
29. That sounds absurd. So where does it come from?

## How we found out there's no universal "now"
30. It starts with one weird fact: light always moves at the same speed, no matter how fast you're moving.
31. That sounds harmless, until you compare it with everything else.
32. Throw a ball forward on a moving train, and to someone on the platform the ball is faster: the train's speed plus your throw.
33. Light doesn't work like that.
34. Shine a torch forward on the same train, and the person on the platform measures that light at exactly the same speed you do.
35. About three hundred thousand kilometres every second. For everyone. Always.
36. It sounds crazy, but it's been tested for over a century. Even light from particles racing at nearly the speed of light still moves at exactly light speed.
37. In 1905, Einstein worked out what this rule forces: if everyone measures the same speed for light, then people moving differently can't agree on time.
38. In particular, they can't agree on what's happening "right now" far away.
39. Picture every place in the universe with its own clock, and your "right now" as a ruler laid across all of them.
40. Stand up, and walk slowly across the room. Your ruler tips, very slightly, compared with someone sitting still.
41. Nearby, the difference is far too small to notice. But it grows with distance.
42. Walk towards the Andromeda galaxy, two and a half million light-years away, and your "right now" over there jumps about four days into its future.
43. Turn around and walk away, and it swings about four days into its past.
44. The physicist Roger Penrose made this famous. It's known as the Andromeda paradox.

## Why beating light breaks cause and effect
45. Normally, this disagreement is harmless.
46. Nothing happening in Andromeda "four days from now" could ever reach you in time, so it can't change anything here.
47. And when one thing can affect another, like a message from a friend, everyone agrees on the order: it's sent before it's read. Causes always come first, however you're moving.
48. But add a shortcut that beats light, and that stops being true.
49. The maths behind this is too involved for this video, so the references are in the description. But the result is simple.
50. Remember how walking tips your ruler? For someone moving fast, it tips a lot. And by their ruler, your faster-than-light message arrives before you sent it. They can reply the same way.
51. Follow their reply back to you, and it arrives before you asked the question. A message into your own past, just like the text from the start.
52. Einstein spotted this in 1907, just two years after he published his theory of relativity. Physicists later nicknamed it the "tachyonic anti-telephone".
53. And our sci-fi shortcuts beat light too, so they can do exactly the same thing. For the warp drive, the physicist Allen Everett showed in 1996 that one warp trip out, and a second one back, can bring you home before you left.
54. The wormhole is even simpler.
55. In 1988, Kip Thorne and his colleagues showed, on paper, that if you take one end of a wormhole on a fast round trip and bring it home, that end comes back younger. It's the famous twin effect.
56. Now the two ends keep different times, years apart. Step into the younger end, and you walk out of the other end, years in the past.
57. And here's the twist: Kip Thorne later helped design the wormhole in Interstellar. The physicist behind the film's shortcut is the one who showed it could be a time machine.
58. So these shortcuts never break the speed limit. They break the order of events.
59. The problem was never speed. It's that there is no universal "now".

## So which one gives?
60. So we have a problem. Einstein's relativity, cause and effect, and faster-than-light shortcuts can't all be true. Something has to give.
61. Either Einstein is wrong somewhere, and there's a hidden universal "now" that no experiment has ever found.
62. Or travel into the past is real, and the universe somehow dodges the paradoxes.
63. Or faster-than-light travel, in any form, just doesn't happen.
64. Nobody has proven it impossible. But most physicists bet on the last one.
65. Stephen Hawking even proposed that the laws of physics always stop a time machine from forming. He joked that it keeps the universe safe for historians.

## Close
66. So the next time you watch Star Wars, Star Trek or Stargate, and someone jumps to hyperspace, goes to warp, or steps through the gate, remember what they're really doing: building a machine that can send that "text from tomorrow".
67. And as far as we know, nobody has ever received one.
68. Because the speed of light isn't just a speed limit.
69. It's the speed of cause and effect.

## Fact-check notes

- **Kip Thorne and Interstellar (2014):** Thorne was the film's scientific consultant and an executive producer; the wormhole and black-hole visuals were built from his equations (see his book *The Science of Interstellar*, 2014).
- **Sci-fi shortcuts as described in-universe:** Star Trek's warp drive distorts space around the ship; Star Wars' hyperspace and Halo's slipspace are alternate dimensions used as shortcuts; Stargate and Interstellar use wormholes.

- **Same speed of light for everyone:** Michelson–Morley (1887) null result; Einstein, special relativity (1905). Direct test of source-independence: Alväger, Farley, Kjellman & Wallin, CERN (1964) — gamma rays from neutral pions moving at 0.99975c measured at c. c ≈ 299,792 km/s.
- **Nearest star:** Proxima Centauri, ~4.2 light-years. **Milky Way:** ~100,000 light-years across (disc diameter; estimates range ~87,000–100,000+).
- **Andromeda:** ~2.5 million light-years. Walking at ~1.4 m/s shifts the simultaneity by v·d/c² ≈ (1.4 / 3×10⁸) × 2.5×10⁶ years ≈ 0.0117 years ≈ 4.3 days. Walking *towards* Andromeda shifts "now" there into its future. Rietdijk (1966), Putnam (1967); popularised by Roger Penrose in *The Emperor's New Mind* (1989) as the "Andromeda paradox".
- **Causal order preserved:** events that can be linked by a signal at or below light speed have the same time order in every inertial frame; only events too far apart for any signal (spacelike separated) can swap order.
- **FTL signal → past:** Einstein (1907) noted superluminal signals would allow effects before causes; Tolman (1917); "tachyonic antitelephone" coined by Benford, Book & Newcomb (1970). The argument uses the relativity principle: if FTL signalling works in one frame, it works in all.
- **Warp drive:** Alcubierre, "The warp drive: hyper-fast travel within general relativity", *Class. Quantum Grav.* 11, L73 (1994). Requires negative energy density (exotic matter). Everett, "Warp drive and causality", *Phys. Rev. D* 53, 7365 (1996): two warp trips can create closed timelike curves.
- **Wormholes:** Morris & Thorne, *Am. J. Phys.* 56, 395 (1988); Morris, Thorne & Yurtsever, "Wormholes, time machines, and the weak energy condition", *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 61, 1446 (1988). Moving one mouth at high speed and returning it creates a time offset between mouths; entering the travelled (younger) mouth exits the stay-at-home mouth in the past. You cannot travel back to before the offset existed.
- **Chronology protection:** Hawking, "Chronology protection conjecture", *Phys. Rev. D* 46, 603 (1992): "…makes the universe safe for historians."
- **Honest framing:** FTL is not proven impossible; the argument shows FTL + relativity ⇒ signals to the past, so most physicists conclude FTL doesn't occur. Option 1 (a preferred frame / hidden "now") is a minority position some physicists do explore.
